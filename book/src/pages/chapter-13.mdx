# Chapter 13: Timing & Optimization (.65 Rule)

The **scan rate** (or scan delay) is the foundational timing parameter in single-switch scanning. Setting the scan rate too fast leads to timing errors and missed items, while setting it too slow creates frustrating "dead time" and lowers text entry rate.

A seminal empirical heuristic developed by Richard Simpson and Heidi Koester (1999, 2013) is the **.65 Rule**:

<div class="figure-card" style="text-align: center; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: bold; color: var(--accent);">
  Optimal Scan Rate (ms) ≈ Mean Reaction Time ÷ 0.65 ≈ 1.54 × RT
</div>

<div class="info-box">
  💡 <strong>In Plain English (The Traffic Light Analogy for the .65 Rule):</strong><br />
  Suppose your client takes 650 milliseconds to react when a light flashes. If you set the scanner speed to 650 ms, it's like a traffic light turning red the exact millisecond a car reaches the stop line—if the driver blinks, they run the light!<br /><br />
  Dividing 650 ms by 0.65 gives 1000 ms (1 full second). That 350 ms extra window is their <strong>safety margin</strong>. It gives them enough time to spot the target, decide, and press comfortably without feeling rushed or missing the item as it passes by.
</div>

### Why 0.65? The Mathematics of Safety Margin

In single-switch auto-scanning, the user must visually perceive the highlight moving onto the desired item, decide to activate, and physically depress the switch before the highlight advances to the next item.

If the scan rate equals the user's mean reaction time (<code>T_scan = RT</code>), the user has a **0% safety margin**—half of all switch presses will land after the highlight has already moved away! 

Dividing by 0.65 provides a ~54% safety buffer, placing the scan rate near the 95th percentile of the user's reaction time distribution. This keeps timing errors below the critical **25% error threshold** (Koester & Simpson, 2014).

### Accounting for Motor Variance (SD)

When a user's reaction times fluctuate significantly due to motor impairment or tremor, calculating scan rate purely from the mean RT is insufficient.

The **Coefficient of Variation (CV)** measures motor consistency:

<div class="figure-card" style="text-align: center; font-weight: bold;">
  CV = Standard Deviation (SD) ÷ Mean Reaction Time (RT)
</div>

- **Low Variance (CV ≤ 0.3):** Standard .65 rule applies: <code>T_scan = RT ÷ 0.65</code>.
- **High Variance (CV > 0.3):** Add a <code>1.65 × SD</code> statistical buffer to account for motor variability:

<div class="figure-card" style="text-align: center; font-size: 1.1rem; font-weight: bold; color: var(--accent);">
  T_scan = (RT + 1.65 × SD) ÷ 0.65
</div>

<div class="highlight-box">
  🩺 <strong>Clinical Rule (Handling Inconsistent Reaction Times):</strong><br />
  Some clients press consistently at 600 ms. Others press at 400 ms when relaxed, but 1200 ms when fatigued or having a muscle spasm. If your client's timing varies wildly (high Standard Deviation), don't just rely on their average speed—add extra safety buffer or enable an <strong>Acceptance Delay</strong> so accidental taps aren't miscounted.
</div>

## Acceptance Time & Selection Confirmation

Acceptance time adds a hold-duration threshold before confirming a switch press, preventing accidental tremors or false triggers from committing an unintended selection.

- **Short (150–300 ms):** Advanced users with refined motor control.
- **Medium (300–600 ms):** Default standard setting.
- **Long (600–1000 ms):** Tremor control for severe spasticity or athetosis.

## Debounce & Post-Acceptance

Mechanical switches can "bounce" electrically upon contact, creating rapid false double presses. A **debounce filter** ignores electrical spikes for 50–100 ms after an initial trigger, while a short **post-acceptance pause** prevents accidental double selections during continuous scanning.

## Timing Guidelines by Clinical User Profile

| User Profile | Recommended Scan Rate | Acceptance Delay | Clinical Rationale |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Beginner / Novice | 1500–2000 ms | 300 ms | Reduces cognitive pressure during initial learning |
| Intermediate | 1000–1500 ms | 300 ms | Optimal balance of speed and motor confidence |
| Advanced | 500–1000 ms | 150–200 ms | Maximizes Text Entry Rate (TER) for skilled users |
| Tremor / Athetosis | 1500–2000 ms | 500–800 ms | Filters unintentional contact and motor spikes |
| Low Vision / AAC | 1000–1500 ms | 300 ms + Audio | Provides time to process auditory scanning prompts |
| Cognitive Impairment | 1500–2500 ms | 300 ms | Extends visual search and decision window |
| Progressive Fatigue (ALS/MND) | AM: 1000 ms / PM: 1500 ms | 300–500 ms | Adapts scan parameters as muscle fatigue increases |
