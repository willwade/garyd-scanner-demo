# Chapter 2: The Bandwidth Problem

**Input Bandwidth** (measured in bits per second or words per minute) refers to the rate of information a user can transmit to a computer system. 

In human-computer interaction (HCI), input modalities exist along a wide bandwidth spectrum:

| Input Modality | Physical Degree of Freedom | Typical Speed | Information Rate |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Touch Typist | 10 fingers, 104 keys | ~80–120 WPM | ~25–35 bits/sec |
| Hunt & Peck Typist | 1–2 fingers, 104 keys | ~15–30 WPM | ~5–10 bits/sec |
| Mouse / Trackpad | 2D Continuous (X/Y + clicks) | ~30–50 WPM | ~12–20 bits/sec |
| Single-Switch User | 1 Binary Channel (ON/OFF) | ~2–15 WPM | ~0.5–2.5 bits/sec |

Single-switch access operates at the **lowest end of the bandwidth spectrum**. Because a switch user cannot directly point to or select from 100+ keys simultaneously, scanning software compensates by **trading time for access**.

<div class="info-box">
  💡 <strong>In Plain English (What "Bandwidth" Means for Your Client):</strong><br />
  Think of input bandwidth like a water pipe. A 10-finger typist has a massive firehose—they can send dozens of letters every second. A single-switch user has a tiny straw. They can only say "YES" (1) or "NO" (0) with one press. Scanning is the clever software trick that lets them build full sentences through that tiny straw, by presenting options one by one over time.
</div>

## The Conveyor Belt Analogy

Imagine sitting at a sushi restaurant where dishes continuously rotate past you on a conveyor belt. You cannot reach across the restaurant to grab any plate instantly. Instead, you must:

1. **Wait** patiently for your desired dish to rotate into your physical reach zone.
2. **Timing:** Activate your movement at precisely the moment the dish arrives.
3. **Miss Penalty:** If you hesitate or activate late, the dish moves away, and you must wait for the next full rotation loop.

<div class="highlight-box">
  🩺 <strong>Clinical Rule: Switch users trade TIME for ACCESS (Shannon, 1948).</strong> Scanning gives single-switch users access to large, complex selection sets—just not instantaneously. Optimizing scan rates and layout structures directly increases transmission bandwidth.
</div>

## Key Takeaway

Scanning is slow but remarkably powerful. By converting spatial choices into a temporal sequence, a single binary switch can control an entire operating system. The goal of scanning design is to maximize bandwidth while minimizing visual search and motor fatigue (Fitts, 1951).
